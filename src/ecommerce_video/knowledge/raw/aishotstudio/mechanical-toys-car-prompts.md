## First Frame Image
A smartphone photo taken from an elevated perspective — roughly 45 to 60 degrees above the desk, as if a person standing or sitting is looking down at the toy on the table in front of them. This angle shows both the top surface and the front/side of the toy simultaneously, revealing its three-dimensional shape and surface details. The desk surface takes up most of the frame — at least 70% of the image is the desk stretching out ahead, with only a thin sliver of room visible at the very top edge of the frame. The desk is a worn white or light-colored surface — mostly empty, with subtle signs of real use like faint scratches, a couple of small scuff marks, maybe a tiny chip in the paint near an edge. A small collectible toy figure sits on the desk in its compact folded-up form — a closed, simple geometric shape — a smooth horizontal capsule lying on its side, roughly the length of a thumb, with rounded domed ends and a perfectly smooth cylindrical middle — with all parts retracted and tucked in. The compact form rests on the desk in the exact orientation it will transform from — its long axis already aligned to the final bus's long axis, so the transformation can unfold in place without the toy needing to tip, roll, or reposition itself. The shape looks like a mysterious abstract object that could transform into anything. A small round metallic silver button is clearly visible on the top center of the toy — this is the transformation trigger. The toy has a highly detailed surface — a classic two-tone vintage paint job split horizontally along the equator of the capsule: the upper half in a soft pearl white with a faint cream warmth, the lower half in a warm sky blue (a gentle teal-leaning aqua) with a subtle metallic flake catching the light. A thin polished chrome trim strip runs along the horizontal seam where the two colors meet, wrapping all the way around the capsule. The white upper half has a barely-there glossy finish while the blue lower half is slightly more matte, giving textural contrast. Tiny sculpted relief details are visible — a small circular VW-style emblem on what will become the front, a faint round headlight indentation on each rounded end, micro rivet details along the chrome strip, and a hint of a sculpted door panel line on each side. A few subtle weathering touches — a barely-there scratch on the white upper section, a tiny paint chip on the chrome trim near one end, faint dust collected along the seam line. Segmented panels and fine mechanical joint lines are visible on the surface suggesting it can transform, but giving no indication of what it becomes. Natural indoor lighting casting a soft shadow beneath and slightly behind the toy. Taken with a phone camera, shallow depth of field — the toy is sharp, the far edge of the desk softly blurs. No text, no watermarks, no logos, no overlays of any kind. Photorealistic.

## End Frame Image
A smartphone photo taken from an elevated perspective — roughly 45 to 60 degrees above the desk, as if a person standing or sitting is looking down at the toy on the table in front of them. This angle shows both the top surface and the front/side of the toy simultaneously, revealing its three-dimensional shape, pose, and surface details from a natural human viewpoint. The desk surface takes up most of the frame — at least 70% of the image is the desk stretching out ahead, with only a thin sliver of room visible at the very top edge of the frame. The desk is a worn white or light-colored surface — mostly empty, with subtle signs of real use like faint scratches, a couple of small scuff marks, maybe a tiny chip in the paint near an edge. The same collectible vintage 1950s VW bus toy figure is now fully transformed and deployed, sitting on the desk in its complete classic split-window VW Type 2 microbus shape with all panels, wheels, and details extended and articulated. The toy occupies the same footprint and orientation on the desk as it did in its compact form — it has expanded outward in place, not relocated. The bus sits squarely on four whitewall tires with polished chrome hubcaps and cream-colored steel wheels, its iconic rounded boxy silhouette unmistakable: a tall friendly front end with the classic split V-windshield divided by a vertical chrome strip, a large round VW emblem centered high on the front, two round chrome-rimmed headlights set low on either side, a wide chrome front bumper with overrider bars, the boxy passenger cabin running back along the body with a row of small square side windows, side cargo doors with sculpted handle details, a sliding side door seam visible on one flank, and a rounded rear engine hood with cooling vents. The elevated angle reveals the full three-dimensional detail of the deployed form — the height of the tall cabin roof, the depth of the wheel wells, the curve of the front face, the faint reflection of the desk in the chrome bumpers. There is no button visible anywhere on the toy — the transformation is complete and the button is now hidden beneath the deployed roof panels. The toy has a highly detailed surface — a classic two-tone vintage paint job: the upper half (cabin roof and upper body above the beltline) in a soft pearl white with a faint cream warmth, the lower half (lower body, fenders, and skirts) in a warm sky blue (a gentle teal-leaning aqua) with a subtle metallic flake catching the light. A thin polished chrome trim strip runs along the horizontal beltline where the two colors meet, sweeping along both sides and wrapping the front and rear. The white upper half has a barely-there glossy finish while the blue lower half is slightly more matte. Sculpted relief details: a small circular VW emblem on the front, round chrome-bezeled headlights, micro rivet details along the chrome trim, sculpted door handles, hinge details on the cargo doors, tiny windshield wipers, a small folding side mirror, faintly tinted transparent windows revealing a hint of cream-colored bench seat interior. Subtle weathering — a barely-there scratch on the white roof section, a tiny paint chip on the chrome trim near the rear, faint dust collected in the panel lines around the doors. Mechanical joints, hinge lines, and segmented armor panels are clearly visible from this angle showing the engineering detail. Natural indoor lighting casting a soft shadow beneath and slightly behind the toy. Taken with a phone camera, shallow depth of field — the toy is sharp, the far edge of the desk softly blurs. No text, no watermarks, no logos, no overlays of any kind. Photorealistic.

## Transition Prompt
A smartphone video filmed handheld with one hand from an elevated angle — roughly 45 to 60 degrees above the desk, looking down and slightly forward, as if a person is standing over the table filming with one hand while the other hand interacts with the toy. The desk surface fills most of the frame. The camera has natural human imperfections — subtle shaky movements, slight tilts, minor focus hunting, small overcorrections as the person adjusts their grip on the phone. The footage feels completely authentic, like someone excitedly recording with one hand on their phone while using their free hand to interact with the toy. The video opens on the toy as shown in the start frame, resting on the desk in its compact capsule form, lying on its side with its long axis aligned forward. The camera drifts slightly then corrects — natural handheld sway. A hand enters the frame from the bottom edge — the person's free hand reaching over the desk. The camera jiggles slightly as their body shifts to reach forward. The hand picks up the compact toy carefully, lifting it just an inch or two off the desk while keeping it in the same horizontal orientation it was resting in — no flipping, no rotating, no tilting. The thumb finds the silver button on top and presses it down with a satisfying tactile click. A brief pause — nothing happens yet. The hand sets the toy back down in the EXACT same spot and EXACT same orientation it picked it up from, then pulls away out of frame. The camera sways gently as the person shifts their weight back. The toy sits motionless on the desk for a half-second beat of anticipation. A faint internal whirring begins — barely audible, like tiny gears engaging. CRITICAL — TRANSFORMATION MUST BE 100% MECHANICAL. NO morphing. NO fading. NO dissolving. NO crossfades. NO parts appearing or disappearing magically. NO smooth shape-blending. Every visible change must be a discrete physical movement: a panel hinging open, a segment sliding out from inside another, a wheel unfolding from a recessed compartment, a part rotating on a visible pivot. Every feature in the final deployed form must trace back to a specific seam, panel, hinge, or compartment that was visible on the compact form — nothing materializes from nowhere. The toy stays in place on the desk throughout — it expands outward into its final form within its existing footprint. It does not jump, lift, roll, or reorient itself. The transformation begins. The capsule's two rounded end caps unlock with a sharp pneumatic hiss and slide outward along the long axis on internal rails — the front cap traveling forward and the rear cap traveling backward, each cap revealing a flat squared-off face as it extends, becoming the front nose and rear engine hood of the bus respectively. As the front cap locks forward with a deep thunk, its rounded dome face splits along a faint vertical seam down the middle and the two halves swing slightly outward and flatten on micro hinges, revealing the iconic split V-windshield with a thin chrome divider snapping up between them with a tiny spring snap. Two round headlight housings rotate forward on tiny hinges from recessed pockets on either side of the front face and click into place, their chrome bezels glinting. A small VW emblem flips up on a center hinge above the windshield with a crisp metallic ping. The cylindrical middle section of the capsule expands upward — the top panel along the white upper half hinges up on a hidden longitudinal seam at the chrome beltline, lifting on both sides simultaneously like a clamshell, then locks vertical to form the tall flat sides of the cabin roof. A separate central roof panel that was nested inside slides up from within on telescoping rails and locks flat on top with a deep thunk, completing the rectangular cabin roof. As the side walls lock vertical, square window panels along the upper body rotate outward and flatten on micro hinges from where they were folded flush against the inner surface, snapping into the row of side windows with sequential ratcheting clicks — front to back, tick-tick-tick-tick. The cargo door seams along both flanks crack open slightly as side panels split along previously-hidden hinge lines, the two halves of the side-door section swinging into their final position with soft servo whirrs and locking flush. Four wheels drop in sequence from clearly recessed wheel wells along the underside of the blue lower half — front-left, front-right, rear-left, rear-right — each wheel swinging down on a hinged suspension arm from its compartment, the whitewall rubber tires touching the desk surface with a light metallic tap as each one locks into place. The chrome hubcaps spin once and settle. The front and rear chrome bumpers slide outward from concealed slots beneath the front and rear caps on telescoping rails, locking into position with crisp metallic clicks, the small overrider bars flicking up on tiny spring hinges with a tiny snap. A folding side mirror flips out from a tiny pocket near the windshield on the driver's side with a soft click. Two tiny windshield wipers rotate up from a recessed slot at the base of the windshield and settle into resting position. Faint internal lighting glows briefly through the now-tinted windows as the cream-colored bench seat interior settles into place inside. During the transformation, the audio is entirely ASMR mechanical sounds — NO music, NO background song, NO narration. The sounds include: precise metallic clicks and snaps as panels lock, soft whirring of tiny servos and gears, ratcheting sounds as window panels deploy in sequence, light tapping as the four wheels contact the desk surface, subtle pneumatic hisses as the capsule end caps slide outward, the satisfying thunk of the roof panel locking flat, the crisp ping of the chrome divider snapping up, tiny spring snaps from the bumper overriders and emblem. Each sound corresponds exactly to the physical movement happening on screen. The overall sound profile is similar to a high-end Transformer toy — tactile, crisp, mechanical, deeply satisfying. The camera reacts naturally throughout the transformation — the person filming flinches slightly when the toy first hisses open, then leans in closer as the cabin roof lifts up, makes a small adjustment when the front bumper extends toward the camera, and settles into a more stable but still handheld framing as the wheels drop and the bus settles onto them. The focus occasionally hunts briefly when the toy changes shape rapidly, then locks back on. The camera stays at the elevated 45-60 degree angle throughout. The transformation completes, matching the end frame exactly. The camera holds with natural micro-movements and breathing sway. The person slowly shifts the camera angle slightly to show off the final bus from a different perspective, perhaps drifting toward the front to admire the split-window face, then settles back. The last sound is a final definitive click as the side mirror locks into position, then silence except for ambient room tone. Throughout the entire video: No text, no watermarks, no logos, no overlays, no music, no narration. Only mechanical ASMR sounds and subtle ambient room noise. The footage must feel 100% like a real person filmed this on their phone — not a produced studio shoot.
